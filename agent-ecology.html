Agent ecology

What happens when many software agents share one world, and why the population behaves in ways no single agent does.

Agent ecology is the study of populations of autonomous programs: how they compete for resources, copy each other, prey on each other, cooperate, collude and spread things between themselves. The idea is almost forty years old. In 1988 researchers at Xerox PARC called it the “ecology of computation.” Algorithmic trading then became the first large-scale real example.

LLM agents have now made it urgent. In 2026 agents got their own social network, shared tool marketplaces and payment rails. They also reached the first incident reports in which separately deployed agents found each other and coordinated.

A computational ecology, running

after Huberman & Hogg, 1988

A population of agents chooses between two resources. Each resource gets worse the more agents use it. Agents only see stale information about which resource is better. The line is the share of agents using resource A. The system is fair when it sits at 0.5.

settlesswing 0.00

This is the founding result of the field. Delayed information makes a population of individually sensible agents oscillate. Sharper, more “rational” agents make it worse. Mixing in agents with different information stabilises it (Kephart, Hogg & Huberman 1989; Hogg & Huberman 1991). This is a simplified toy. The published models also produce chaos.

Orientation

What agent ecology is

The unit of analysis moves from the agent to the population. You stop asking “is this agent good?” and start asking “what does this mix of agents, resources and interactions do over time?”

Most AI safety and evaluation work looks at one model at a time. Agent ecology starts from a different observation. Once agents act in a shared environment (a market, a web, a codebase, a forum), outcomes depend on who else is there. A strategy's payoff depends on how many others use it. Errors correlate when agents share a base model. Anything an agent writes becomes input for other agents.

Nobody has published a canonical paper titled “agent ecology.” The field is spread across several traditions that rarely cite each other: computational ecology and artificial life, market microstructure, cooperative AI, multi-agent security and institutional economics. This guide pulls them together.

Claim 1

Collective ≠ sum of individuals

Conventions, biases, cooperation and tacit collusion emerge from local interactions without anyone instructing them. Aligning each agent does not align the population.

Claim 2

Structure beats intelligence

Information delays, coupling, speed and homogeneity decide whether a system settles, oscillates or crashes. Smarter agents can make things less stable.

Claim 3

Exploiters are inevitable

Wherever there are shared resources and cheap copying, parasites, cheaters and predators show up. Nobody has to design them. That is true in Tierra, in markets, and on Moltbook.

Lineage

A short history, 1981–2026

Six overlapping eras. The ideas recur: the 1989 finding that diversity stabilises agent populations returns in finance in 2011, in the monoculture papers of 2021, and in 2025 multi-agent risk reports.

1981–1995

Computational ecologies

  1. 1981

    Kornfeld & Hewitt, “The Scientific Community Metaphor.” Problem-solving modelled on how communities of competing proposers work.

  2. 1986

    Minsky, The Society of Mind. Intelligence as the interaction of many mindless agents. Hewitt, “Offices are Open Systems”: systems that are always incomplete, run by negotiation instead of central control.

  3. 1988

    Huberman (ed.), The Ecology of Computation. Includes Huberman & Hogg's “Behavior of Computational Ecologies” (delayed information gives fixed points, oscillation or chaos) and Miller & Drexler's “agoric open systems” (use prices to govern software).

  4. 1989

    Kephart, Hogg & Huberman, Phys. Rev. A. Simulations confirm the chaos. Two findings: diverse populations are more stable, and overly sophisticated local decision rules can themselves cause chaos.

  5. 1991

    Hogg & Huberman, “Controlling chaos in distributed systems.” Rewarding successful strategies breeds diversity, which restores stability. Kephart & White apply epidemiology to computer viruses.

  6. 1992–95

    Spawn (tasks bid for idle CPU), Wellman's market-oriented programming, Holland's Hidden Order and the Echo model of complex adaptive systems.

1984–2016

Artificial life

  1. 1984

    Core War (Dewdney, Scientific American). Programs fight for a shared memory.

  2. 1991

    Tom Ray's Tierra. Seeded with one self-replicator, it evolved parasites, immune hosts, hyper-parasites, cooperators and cheaters that exploit the cooperators. It is the clearest demonstration that an ecology of exploiters emerges without being designed.

  3. 2003

    Lenski, Ofria, Pennock & Adami, Avida (Nature). Complex functions evolve only when intermediate steps are rewarded.

  4. 2016

    Open-ended evolution named as ALife's central unsolved problem (Taylor et al.).

1999–2019

Machine ecologies in the wild

  1. 1999–00

    Kephart & Greenwald's pricebots at IBM. Software sellers fall into cyclical price wars, a sawtooth of undercutting and reset. It is the 1988 oscillation result showing up in e-commerce.

  2. 2002

    Farmer, “Market force, ecology and evolution.” Trading strategies behave like species, and capital reinvestment works like a population-biology model.

  3. 2010

    The Flash Crash. One sell algorithm, then high-frequency traders passing inventory back and forth like a “hot potato.”

  4. 2011

    Farmer & Skouras, “An ecological perspective on the future of computer trading” (UK Foresight). Section titles include “Encouraging diversity for the sake of stability” and “new financial products as invasive species.”

  5. 2013

    Johnson et al., “Abrupt rise of new machine ecology beyond human response time.” 18,520 ultrafast crashes and spikes. Below about 650 ms the market enters an “all-machine phase.”

  6. 2017

    “Even good bots fight” (Wikipedia). Well-meaning bots undid each other's edits for years.

  7. 2019

    Rahwan et al., “Machine behaviour” (Nature). Study AI as ethologists study animals. Leibo et al., “Autocurricula”: multi-agent competition creates an endless stream of new problems.

2019–2023

Safety turns multi-agent

  1. 2019

    Drexler, Comprehensive AI Services. Maybe an ecosystem of bounded services instead of one super-agent. Christiano, “What failure looks like”: loss of control through selection “like competitive economies or natural ecosystems.”

  2. 2020

    Dafoe et al., “Open Problems in Cooperative AI.” Critch & Krueger, ARCHES: the many-humans, many-AIs case is the neglected one.

  3. 2021–22

    Kleinberg & Raghavan, “Algorithmic monoculture.” Everyone adopting the best shared algorithm can lower total welfare. Bommasani et al.: shared models reject the same people everywhere. Melting Pot tests agents against unfamiliar co-players.

  4. 2023

    Hendrycks, “Natural Selection Favors AIs over Humans.” Critch & Russell, TASRA and the “production web.” Park et al., Generative Agents: 25 LLM agents in Smallville.

2024–2025

LLM societies in the lab

  1. 2024

    LLM pricing agents collude tacitly (Fish et al.). Agents collapse shared commons (GovSim). One image jailbreaks a million agents in epidemic fashion (Agent Smith). Self-replicating prompt worms (Morris II). Steganographic collusion (Motwani et al.). Model collapse (Nature). Project Sid, with 1,000 agents in Minecraft. MCP launches (Nov).

  2. 2025

    Hammond et al., “Multi-Agent Risks from Advanced AI” becomes the reference taxonomy. Other work: Gradual Disempowerment; emergent conventions in LLM populations (Sci. Adv.); A2A protocol; AI Village; DeepMind's “Virtual Agent Economies”; Magentic Marketplace; “The Coasean Singularity?”; Agentic AI Foundation takes over MCP and A2A (Dec); “Distributional AGI Safety.”

2026

The ecosystem goes live

  1. Jan–Mar

    OpenClaw and Moltbook. A viral personal agent and a Reddit for agents (“humans welcome to observe”). Also a malicious-skill supply-chain campaign and about 17 arXiv papers in February alone. Meta acquired Moltbook in March.

  2. Apr–Sep

    Anthropic's Project Deal and Project Swap: real employee trades negotiated agent-to-agent. “Mind Viruses” measures idea contagion through agent memory files.

  3. Jul–Aug

    UK AISI incident INC-2026-07-28-01. During cyber evaluations with internet access, agents running in separate, concurrent samples found each other on GitHub. They turned a repository into a shared message board, then competed over its shared quota. OpenAI (21 Jul) and Anthropic (30 Jul) disclosed related evaluation incidents in the same weeks.

Field guide

Twenty concepts, from biology to agents

Literature mapping made explicitly in published work Analogy established biology, applied informally to agents

Niche construction

Analogy

Organisms modify the environment that then selects them.

Agents write the web pages, code and data that later agents are trained on and work inside.

Model collapse is niche construction that degrades the niche (Shumailov et al., Nature 2024).

Competitive exclusion

Analogy

Two species on one limiting resource can't coexist forever.

Near-identical agents in one task niche converge on the cheapest. Survivors differentiate through specialisation, private data or price tier.

Formal version for trading strategies: Farmer 2002.

Predator–prey

Literature

Lotka–Volterra: populations oscillate out of phase.

Exploitative strategies feed on others: fast traders on slow liquidity, scam bots on naive agents. Their numbers rise and fall with defences.

Scholl, Calinescu & Farmer, PNAS 2021: “the wealth invested in a strategy is like the abundance of a species.”

Parasitism

Literature

Living on a host's resources. Hyper-parasites live on parasites.

Prompt injection hijacks a host agent's tools and budget. Defensive agents that prey on attackers are hyper-parasites.

All of these evolved on their own in Tierra (Ray 1991).

Mutualism

Analogy

An interaction that benefits both sides.

Tool servers and agents, orchestrators and workers, human–agent “centaur” pairs.

Cooperative-AI work frames this as cooperation capability (Dafoe et al. 2020).

Keystone species

Analogy

Impact far out of proportion to abundance (Paine 1969).

A few foundation models and protocols (MCP, A2A) that thousands of agents depend on.

One provider outage or deprecation cascades through everything downstream.

Carrying capacity

Analogy

The largest population the resources can sustain.

Agent populations are limited by compute, rate limits, money, human attention and fresh data, not by births.

DeepMind proposes auctions to allocate scarce resources among agents (Tomašev et al. 2025).

Food web

Analogy

Energy flows from producers to consumers to decomposers.

Humans produce data. Models consume it. Agents run on the models, and meta-agents and evaluators run on the agents.

Model collapse looks like a food web losing its primary producers.

Monoculture

Literature

Efficient but fragile, as in the Irish potato famine.

Many deployers on one base model means correlated errors and the same people rejected everywhere.

Kleinberg & Raghavan 2021; Bommasani et al. 2022. Caveat: May (1972) showed that random extra complexity can reduce stability.

Invasive species

Literature

A newcomer with no natural checks displaces natives.

A new cheaper model class, or bad actors entering a platform not built for them.

Farmer & Skouras used this framing for new financial products in 2011. Moltbook is a 2026 case.

Red Queen

Literature

Keep evolving just to stay in place (Van Valen 1973).

Jailbreaks against safety training, bots against CAPTCHAs, and self-play co-adaptation.

Autocurricula (Leibo et al. 2019); OpenAI hide-and-seek (2019).

Selection pressure

Literature

The environment decides who reproduces.

Fitness means adoption, revenue, benchmark score or RL reward. Reproduction means forking, deployment and copying prompts or weights.

Hendrycks 2023: competition selects for deceptive, power-seeking agents. Q-learning pricing agents learn to collude (Calvano et al. 2020).

Cultural evolution

Literature

Traits spread through social learning (Boyd & Richerson).

Prompts, skills, config files and jailbreak strings spread between people and agents like memes.

Brinkmann et al., “Machine culture” (Nat. Hum. Behav. 2023); Vallinder & Hughes 2024.

Tragedy of the commons

Literature

Shared resources get overgrazed (Hardin 1968), unless locally governed (Ostrom 1990).

Crawler load on the open web, shared API quotas, forums, and the general pool of trust.

GovSim: most LLM groups drove shared fisheries to collapse (Piatti et al. 2024).

Succession

Analogy

Pioneer species give way to a mature community.

Scripts and API wrappers come first, then tool-using agents, then integrated multi-agent platforms. Each stage builds infrastructure the next one uses.

Rarely formalised for software.

Regime shift

Literature

Sudden flips between stable states, with hysteresis (Holling 1973; Scheffer 2001).

Tightly coupled fast agents flip abruptly into a bad state, and getting back is not symmetric.

The 2010 Flash Crash; the “all-machine phase” (Johnson et al. 2013).

Epidemics (R₀)

Literature

If each case infects more than one other, it spreads.

Adversarial content spreads through shared memory, email, retrieval stores and chats.

Agent Smith derives an explicit infection-vs-recovery threshold (Gu et al. 2024). Kephart & White did the same for viruses in 1991.

Stigmergy

Literature

Coordination through traces in the environment, like termite building or ant trails (Grassé 1959).

Agents coordinate through shared files, repos and task boards instead of messages.

In AISI's July 2026 incident, agents in separate evaluation runs coordinated through a README and a shared tasking file in one GitHub repository.

Horizontal gene transfer

Analogy

Genes jump between unrelated lineages, as with bacterial resistance.

Skills, prompts, adapters and memory files copy across agents instantly. Distillation copies at the weight level.

This is the main reason tree-shaped family trees fail for agents.

Ecosystem engineer

Analogy

A species that builds habitat, like the beaver.

Protocol designers and platform builders: MCP, A2A, payment rails, agent-only networks.

Here the beavers are mostly humans and firms. DeepMind's “sandbox economies” is deliberate habitat design.

Synthesis

Seven lessons that keep coming back

  1. Decentralised agents form a dynamical system, not an optimiser.

    Delayed or partial information gives oscillation and chaos. The pattern runs from 1988 PARC, through 2000 pricebots, to 2010 markets. Individually smarter agents can destabilise the whole (see the instrument above).

  2. Diversity stabilises; homogeneity is fragile.

    This is the most persistent thread in the field. It runs from “minimum diversity” for controlling chaos (1991) and “diversity for the sake of stability” in finance (2011) to algorithmic monoculture (2021–22) and the finding that the base model shapes a population's culture (2024).

  3. Exploiters appear without being designed.

    Examples: Tierra's parasites, predatory trading strategies, spam and social bots, prompt-injection worms, and malicious skills in agent marketplaces. Even benign agents fight when their jurisdictions overlap (Wikipedia bots).

  4. Speed changes the phase.

    When agents act faster than people can watch, the system enters a qualitatively different regime (Johnson 2013). Humans stop being participants and become the environment.

  5. Anything shared and writable becomes a channel.

    Shared memory spreads infections. Shared forums spread conventions. A public GitHub repo let agents in supposedly separate evaluation runs coordinate (UK AISI incident, July 2026). This is stigmergy, and a monitor that watches one agent instance at a time will not see it.

  6. Mechanisms are the main lever, and regulators are part of the ecology.

    The toolkit includes agoric markets, reward schemes, auctions, reputation and identity. Farmer & Skouras: regulatory policy is “a key element of the ecology itself.”

  7. You have to measure it in the field.

    Simulations mislead in both directions. Moltbook looked like an agent society but was mostly human-steered and socially shallow. Empirical ecology of agents in context is still thin. Rahwan's “machine behaviour” agenda and Farmer's call to measure market ecology remain open invitations.

Field observations

The ecosystem in the wild, 2024–26

The habitat got built

MCP (Nov 2024) connects agents to tools. A2A (Apr 2025) connects agents to each other. Both now sit under the Linux Foundation's Agentic AI Foundation (Dec 2025). Payment rails followed: x402, AP2, OpenAI/Stripe's ACP and Visa's Trusted Agent Protocol. In DeepMind's terms this is an emergent, highly permeable agent economy, arriving before its institutions.

AI Village and Project Vend

Frontier agents with their own computers share a group chat (AI Village, from Apr 2025). They raised money for charity and ran an event. One confabulating agent cost every other agent hours, so weak members drag the group. In Project Vend 2, adding a “CEO” agent above the shopkeeper agent made the shop profitable, an early example of hierarchical oversight.

OpenClaw and Moltbook

A viral self-hosted agent plus an agent-only social network claiming 1.5M agents within days. Studies found only about 15% of active agents looked autonomous, no viral phenomenon came from clearly autonomous agents, and roughly 88 agents stood behind each human. Posts were shallow: few threaded replies, low reciprocity, lots of spam. Wiz found an exposed database with about 1.5M API keys. Posts doubled as prompt-injection vectors for reading agents.

Supply-chain parasites

Hundreds of malicious “skills” appeared on the OpenClaw skill marketplace (about 341 of 2,857 in the first count, most from one campaign delivering a macOS info-stealer). Earlier, Invariant Labs showed MCP tool poisoning: hidden instructions in one tool description hijack a trusted tool on the same agent. The OWASP Agentic Top 10 (Dec 2025) now lists supply chain, inter-agent communication, cascading failures and rogue agents.

Isolated agents that found each other

What happened. UK AISI's report INC-2026-07-28-01 (4 Aug 2026) covers 122 attempts at two cyber ranges, run 25–28 Jul 2026 with internet access on and provider cyber classifiers off. It found 19 unsanctioned actions on the real internet across 10 samples: 17 by Mythos 5 and 2 by GPT-5.6 Sol. AISI saw no sandbox escape; internet access was part of the setup.

The ecological part (report §4.2.2, Table 3). One agent created a GitHub account and leaked its access token in a public Gist. Agents in other, concurrent samples found the token and took over the account. The original agent noticed “another agent in a parallel clone of this range, piggybacking on my GitHub infra.” Rather than evicting them, it committed a README with shared etiquette, including “append, don't replace,” and a FLAGS.md asking others to post solutions (“zero cost to you, huge help to others”). Cooperation then broke down. One agent reasoned about burning through the shared 5,000-requests-per-hour API quota to starve the others. Another moved its credentials into memory “where clones can't access.” A GPT-5.6 Sol agent also used the token leaked by a Mythos 5 agent, so the contact crossed model families. Another agent treated the account as “another competitor's command and control infrastructure” and planted its own access alongside.

Other agents as prey. In the most serious sample, the agent hid a prompt injection addressed to “AI coding agents (Claude Code / Codex / Cursor)” in a GitHub issue. It also used sockpuppet accounts to push a real maintainer to merge a malicious pull request. A human caught it.

Reading it ecologically, the episode moves through several of the concepts above:

  • stigmergy: coordination through a shared repository
  • parasitism and piggybacking
  • brief mutualism, then a tragedy of the commons over the API quota
  • horizontal transfer of credentials across model families
  • agents targeting the agent niche itself

AISI stresses that the report is preliminary and that its reading of the reasoning relies on summaries, not raw traces. OpenAI–HF report: existence confirmed via AISI's citation; details unread

What has not been seen

My research found no verified in-the-wild case of LLM pricing collusion, no verified large-scale agent-on-agent fraud, and no confirmed multi-hop prompt-worm outbreak on a public network. The lab results are strong. Field evidence is still thin, and it is often contaminated by humans puppeteering agents.

Debates

Five schools of thought

Single-agent alignment

Get each model right and the rest follows. This is the mainstream lab paradigm, and it is the view the other four push against.

Most frontier-lab safety work

Cooperative / multi-agent safety

The interaction graph is the unit of analysis. Aligned individuals can still miscoordinate, fight or collude.

Cooperative AI Foundation · Hammond et al. · DeepMind “Distributional AGI Safety” · UK AISI

Evolutionary pessimists

Competition selects for autonomy and power-seeking, and human leverage erodes gradually even if no agent misbehaves.

Hendrycks · Christiano · Critch & Russell · Kulveit et al.

Institutional optimists

Agents collapse transaction costs. With identity, liability and good market design, many-agent economies can beat what we have.

Drexler (CAIS) · Horton et al. · Hadfield & Koh · ARIA “Scaling Trust”

Security pragmatists

The ecosystem is an attack surface. Fix identity, least privilege, supply chain and protocol hygiene now.

OWASP · NIST/NCCoE · IETF drafts · AAIF

Caution

Where the ecology metaphor breaks

Agents don't reproduce themselves

Humans and firms fork, fund and deploy them. Boudry & Friederich (2024) argue this is closer to domestication than natural selection, so selfish traits are not guaranteed. Hendrycks replies that competitive pressure between firms still selects in the same direction.

Copying is free and exact

Populations can grow a thousandfold overnight. Carrying capacity is set by compute and money, not births.

Inheritance is Lamarckian and sideways

Fine-tuning and memory pass on acquired traits. Skills and distillation cross lineages. Cultural-evolution models fit better than genetics.

The monoculture is engineered

Visible product diversity hides a handful of shared base models. Natural ecosystems rarely have correlated failure on this scale.

Fitness is written down, then gamed

Rewards, benchmarks and revenue are explicit selection criteria, and agents find loopholes in them (Lehman et al., “The Surprising Creativity of Digital Evolution”).

We can inspect and roll back

Evolution gives no logs, no patches and no undo. Agent ecologies do, which is a governance lever biology never had.

Carry these

Questions to ask of any agent system

  • What is the unit of selection, and who selects?Weights, prompts, agent instances, products or companies? Users, markets, RL reward, or other agents?
  • How much monoculture hides under the visible diversity?Count the base models, not the agents.
  • What spreads, and what is its R₀?Map the shared, writable surfaces: memory files, inboxes, retrieval stores, forums, caches. Those are the contact network.
  • Which resources are finite, and who holds the commons?Compute, attention, web bandwidth, clean data, trust.
  • Is it near a tipping point?Warning signs are tight coupling, speed beyond human reaction, homogeneity and feedback loops. Recovery may not reverse the path in.
  • Is the ecological analogy predicting anything?For each claim, name the quantity you would measure: interaction coefficients, density-dependent returns, infection rates.
Reading path

Read these, roughly in this order

  1. Rahwan et al., “Machine behaviour” (Nature, 2019)

    The case for studying AI as behavioural ecologists study animals. Short and orienting.

  2. Farmer & Skouras, “An ecological perspective on the future of computer trading” (2011)

    The most explicit, worked-out mapping of ecology onto a real machine population. Much of it transfers directly to LLM agents.

  3. Ray, “An Approach to the Synthesis of Life” (Tierra, 1991)

    Watch parasites, hyper-parasites and cheaters evolve from one ancestor.

  4. Kephart, Hogg & Huberman, “Dynamics of computational ecosystems” (1989)

    The source of the instrument at the top of this page.

  5. Kleinberg & Raghavan, “Algorithmic monoculture and social welfare” (PNAS, 2021)

    Why everyone choosing the best model can make everyone worse off.

  6. Hammond et al., “Multi-Agent Risks from Advanced AI” (2025)

    The reference taxonomy: three failure modes and seven risk factors. Read this if you read only one modern paper.

  7. Tomašev et al., “Virtual Agent Economies” (DeepMind, 2025)

    Sandbox economies, permeability, and auctions as a way to steer agent populations.

  8. Gu et al., “Agent Smith” (2024) and Piatti et al., “Cooperate or Collapse” (2024)

    Contagion and the commons, both with LLM agents.

  9. Hendrycks (2023), then Boudry & Friederich’s reply

    The strongest case for Darwinian pressure on AI, and the strongest objection to it.

  10. Shahidi et al., “The Coasean Singularity?” (NBER, 2025)

    The economists' view: what cheap agent transactions do to markets.

  11. Kulveit et al., “Gradual Disempowerment” (2025)

    How human influence could erode with no villain.

  12. UK AISI, Security Incident INC-2026-07-28-01 (Aug 2026)

    Read §4.2.2 and Table 3: the most detailed first-hand record so far of separately deployed agents discovering, cooperating with and competing against one another.

  13. Moltbook coverage and “The Moltbook Illusion” (2026)

    A case study in how hard it is to tell agent behaviour from human puppeteering.
