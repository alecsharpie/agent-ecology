How Crowds Make Up Their Minds

Twenty-four small language models play a game with one rule: match your partner. Simple rule-following agents agree within twenty rounds. Small language models never do; bigger ones do. The difference turns out to be one ordinary habit.

1. A game with one rule

Why do we drive on the right? Not because anyone worked out that the right is better. Enough people did it that doing anything else became costly, and then everyone did it. A convention is a shared answer to a question with no right answer, and nobody in particular has to pick it. It can appear from many private, local interactions, and that is what emergence means: a pattern in the whole that no part designed.

The naming game was introduced as a model of how language conventions spread (Steels 1995; Baronchelli et al. 2006). Centola and colleagues ran it with people in 2015 and 2018.

The naming game is the smallest laboratory for this. A population of agents is paired up at random, round after round. In each pair both agents secretly pick a name from the same list. If they pick the same one, both score; if not, both lose a little. Nobody is told which name to use, and nobody sees the whole population. Each agent remembers only its own last few games.

In 2025, Ashery, Aiello and Baronchelli put large language models into this game and found that populations of them do settle on shared names. They also found that the population can favour a name no single model prefers, and that a small committed minority can overturn an established convention. We tried to reproduce that with models small enough to run in a browser tab: Qwen2.5 at 0.5 and 1.5 billion parameters, and Llama 3.2 at 1 billion. This is the exact message every agent received:

2. Agents that copy

Before any language model, it helps to see what success looks like. Our reference agents follow one rule: play the name your last five partners played most often. They see exactly what a language-model agent sees and nothing more.

The curve starts flat. For a while every name has a few supporters and nothing moves much. Then one name, by luck, gets slightly more partners talking about it. Agents who copy their partners start playing it, which gives it more partners still, and the curve shoots up. That positive feedback is the engine of every convention in this article. Keep it in mind, because it is exactly what the language models lack.

3. A habit playground

Copying the majority of your last five partners is one strategy among many. To compare agents, including language models, it helps to boil any strategy down to three habits about the last round only:

  • Repeat after a win. If I matched my partner, how often do I play the same name again?
  • Copy after a loss. If I didn't match, how often do I switch to my partner's name?
  • Explore after a loss. How often do I try some other name at random? Whatever is left over, I keep my own name.

Below, a population of 24 agents plays with whatever habits you set. The presets are the habits we measured in real language-model runs (section 4). Try them, then try to find the recipe that works.

A few rules of thumb fall out of playing with it, and a systematic sweep confirms them (section 5). Always repeat a winning name: any wandering after a win puts a ceiling on agreement. Copy sometimes, not always: copying 30–50% of the time works best. At 90%, two mismatched agents both copy each other, swap names and mismatch again. Almost never explore: with ten names, even 10% random exploration keeps re-seeding rival names faster than one can win.

4. What small language models do

We ran five pre-registered conditions with five seeds each: Qwen2.5 1.5B with sampling (temperature 0.7) and with greedy decoding (each agent an exact clone); Qwen2.5 0.5B; Llama 3.2 1B; and a mixed population of half Qwen and half Llama. That's 24,000 model calls, all on a laptop GPU through WebGPU.

We wrote down predictions before the runs, including "the 1.5B model converges in most seeds". They are graded in the methods section. Most were wrong.

5. The missing habit

Why do the language models stall? The most direct way to find out is to stop watching populations and ask one agent about one situation. We gave the model a scripted memory and sampled its answer 100 times, rotating the names through all ten letters and shuffling the list every call.

Now we can put every model on one map. The background below is a sweep of simple habit agents, like the playground, run 16 times per cell for 60 rounds: how much agreement each combination of "repeat after a win" and "copy after a loss" reaches. On top are the habits we measured in each real run.

The map tells a sharper story than "small models are bad at this". Each model, under each prompt, fails in its own way:

  • Qwen 1.5B is too stubborn. It sits high on "repeat after a win", but far left on "copy". Camps form in the first few rounds and never dissolve. With greedy decoding it is even more extreme, and the populations freeze solid.
  • Llama 1B is too fickle. It drops even a winning name two times out of three, so nothing accumulates.
  • Qwen 0.5B copies the most, about 22% of the time, more than its larger sibling. But it also abandons winners half the time.

What none of these small models does, under any wording, is the thing every converging population needs: adopt a partner's name after losing to it. Is that a matter of size? We climbed the ladder to find out.

A correction we had to make

We first wrote this up as "Qwen is stubborn, Llama is fickle", a difference between models. Then we ran the check the plan required: the same game with a second wording of the prompt. With the second wording the same Qwen model abandons a winning name 88% of the time, as fickle as Llama. Stubborn or fickle depends on the model and the prompt. That's why the "plain wording" dot sits next to Llama on the map. What held under every wording and name list we tried: copying stays rare.

6. Where copying switches on

We repeated the probes on bigger models: Qwen2.5 at 3 and 7 billion parameters (still in the browser), and Google's open Gemma 4 at 26 and 31 billion (through the Gemini API). Same prompt, same scripted memories.

Gemma 4 31B plays the textbook strategy: keep a winner, follow the partners' majority after losses, and don't overreact to one bad round. That is the rule agent from section 2, which the model arrived at from the prompt alone. So does a population of them converge?

Two results here answer the question from section 5. First, size matters, but it isn't the whole story. Qwen 7B has the copying habit, but a prompt that lists "you picked X" five times buries it; take those lines out and the same model converges. At 1.5B, taking them out reveals nothing: the model just guesses. Second, the populations that converge have exactly the habits the playground predicts. Gemma repeats a winning name 100% of the time, copies its partner about a quarter of the time after a loss, and almost never explores. You can load those habits in the hero figure at the top. Both successful runs are also in the replay in section 4.

7. Where the winning name comes from

The populations never fully agree, but they do lean. In four of five Qwen 1.5B runs, Q led. Where does Q come from? We asked 200 fresh agents, each with an empty memory and a freshly shuffled list, to pick a name.

So the group's lean is inherited from individual taste. But the group amplifies it: Q is 28% of solo picks, yet it led most populations. The mechanism shows up when we ask whom agents copy. Split every "I lost last round" moment by what the partner played:

The same amplification appears in the rule agents with no language model at all. We gave each rule agent a weak lean toward F: on its own it picks F 12% of the time instead of about 10%. F then won 26% of the populations. A preference too weak to notice in any individual more than doubles at the level of the group. The paper we replicated reports group biases that no individual holds. With small models, we found something simpler but just as important: individual biases that the group magnifies.

Gemma is the clean test of the paper's version. On its own it has no favourite: all 200 fresh agents picked whichever name was listed first, so the shuffle spreads its picks evenly across the letters. Its three populations settled on F, X and Z, three different names. That is what you would expect with no collective bias. Three runs can't rule a bias out; it would take dozens of seeds, about 40 minutes each on the free API tier.

9. Herding into the wrong answer

The naming game rewards following the crowd, since any shared name will do. Sometimes following the crowd is a mistake. The classic demonstration is the urn game (Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer and Welch 1992; Anderson and Holt 1997). A coin picks one of two urns: one holds two red balls and one blue, the other two blue and one red. Players go one at a time. Each privately draws a ball, which points to the right urn two times in three. Each sees everyone's earlier guesses, but not their balls, then guesses publicly.

A perfectly rational player follows their own ball until the earlier guesses lead by two, then follows the crowd whatever they drew. That's correct for them, but their guess now carries no information, so the next player is in the same spot, and so on forever. This is an information cascade. With ten players, about one game in five locks into the wrong urn, even though everyone reasons perfectly. Our simulation gives 18.7%, against a theoretical 19.2%.

Naming the urns "A" and "B" left Qwen 1.5B at chance: its first guess matched its own ball 52% of the time. Naming them by colour ("the red urn") fixed that. Capability floors decide which social effects you can even observe.

We put language models in the players' seats, with the urns named by colour. Then we asked how the chance of going against your own ball depends on how many earlier players disagreed with it:

Gradual conformity sounds mild. Its effect on the group is not. Below, you can simulate 2,000 ten-player games using each player type's measured behaviour: how often player 1 follows its own ball, and the conformity curve above.

That last case is the cleanest lesson in the whole project: a sensible rule for using other people's choices does not help if the first choices are systematically off. It amplifies them. Every player after the first saw more information than the first, and the group ended up worse than if nobody had looked.

10. Two more classics

Two older results round out the picture, both about the gap between what individuals want and what the group produces. Both use simple rules, no language models.

Schelling's segregation model (1971)

Two kinds of agents live on a grid. Each is content if at least a share t of its neighbours are its own kind; discontented agents move to a random empty cell. At t = 30%, every agent is happy to be a one-in-three minority. Nobody wants segregation.

The El Farol bar (Arthur 1994)

A hundred people decide each week whether to go to a bar that is fun only if 60 or fewer turn up. There's no right forecast: if everyone expects a quiet night, everyone goes. Each agent holds a few simple forecasting rules ("same as last week", "average of the last four weeks", "mirror of last week") and uses whichever has been most accurate lately.

11. What we learned

  1. Emergence needs a feedback loop. Conventions form when agents adopt what they see others doing, which makes it more visible, which gets it adopted more. Remove copying and nothing emerges, however capable the agents.
  2. Small language models don't copy their partners. They mostly repeat their own past choice, or wander at random, depending on the prompt. Not one of 25 populations converged.
  3. Copying switches on with size, and then conventions form. Qwen 7B copies once its own past picks are out of the prompt; Gemma 4 26B copies reliably, and all three of its populations converged, tipped by a committed minority between 12.5% and 21%.
  4. Habits belong to model and prompt together. The same model was stubborn under one wording and fickle under another. Any claim about "how a model behaves socially" has to survive a change of wording.
  5. Groups amplify individual biases. Each model's populations drifted to its own favourite letters, because agents copy names they already like more readily. A small individual tilt becomes the group's direction.
  6. Tipping points are cliffs. Overturning a convention takes a fairly fixed fraction of committed agents, 13–29% depending on memory. Below that nothing happens; just above it the flip is nearly certain.
  7. Social information can make a group worse. Language-model players who conform gradually end up less accurate than players who ignore each other. Even perfectly rational herding amplifies a biased first guess.

Methods, predictions and mistakes

Setup. All models ran locally in Chrome on WebGPU through WebLLM, as 4-bit quantised builds, about 0.3–1.2 seconds per call on an M3 laptop. Every call was logged: the shuffled list, the raw output, the parsed name, the payoff and the timing. All metrics come from those logs, so every number here can be recomputed without running a model. Decoding was constrained to a JSON schema whose only allowed values are the ten names, and there were zero parse failures in about 35,000 calls.

Pre-registration. Five predictions were frozen in the repository before any model result was analysed (the first seed was at round 6 of 40 when they were frozen, a fact we logged at the time).

A measurement caveat. Our "converged" bar (above 90% on one name for five rounds) may be out of reach for any agent that samples its answers. An agent that drops a winning name 14% of the time leaves about 3 of 24 agents off-convention every round, and the bar allows at most 2. We kept the pre-registered rule and report the level of agreement alongside it.

Mistakes we caught. A dev-server setting meant results saved after start-up were never served, which would have silently restarted interrupted runs from round 1. One cascade run was hot-reloaded mid-way by an edit to its code; its last 12 games used a changed prompt and are excluded. A first back-of-envelope theory for cascades was off by 2.5 standard errors, because a cascade formed by the last two players has nobody after them to reveal it. A metric helper passed an array index where it expected a threshold. Tests caught that one. All of this is in the repository's journal.

Follow-ups after the grid (exploratory, not pre-registered). Qwen2.5 3B and 7B ran in the browser like the others; 7B takes about 4 seconds a call. Gemma 4 26B and 31B ran through the Gemini API on its free tier, paced at 25 requests a minute, about 7 seconds per call. We also tried Gemini 3.8 Flash as a frontier anchor, but the free tier allows 20 requests a day, and one population needs 960.

Limitations. Mostly small models, one name pool of letters (and one run with nonsense words), 40 rounds per run, five seeds per pre-registered condition and one to three for the follow-ups. Tipping on Gemma is one run per setting. The paper we replicated used larger models and longer games. Our results map where its findings start to hold; they don't say it is wrong.
